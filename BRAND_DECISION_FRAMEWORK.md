# ALTERNATIVE™ BRAND DECISION FRAMEWORK
**Document:** Creative and Production Decision Governance
**Program Director:** Claude (AI Production System)
**Date:** 2026-06-26
**Version:** 1.0
**Authority:** This framework governs every major creative and production decision made in this program. It supersedes task-level instructions when a task-level instruction would produce a weaker outcome for the brand. When a conflict exists between completing a task and strengthening the brand, this framework is consulted first.

---

## PREAMBLE

A label is not a container for information. It is a sales tool, a brand signal, and a promise made at arm's length to a consumer who has not yet decided to trust you. Every decision made on this label — every point size, every tracking value, every millimeter of space between hierarchy levels, every choice about what appears and what does not — either builds or erodes the brand's ability to make that promise credibly.

ALTERNATIVE™ is attempting something genuinely difficult: launching a super-premium brand in a category that is new, legally complicated, socially contested, and visually underdeveloped. Most brands in this space look like what they are. ALTERNATIVE™ must look like what it intends to become. That gap — between current category norms and the brand's intended position — is where every design decision either wins or loses.

This framework exists to ensure that every decision in this program is made consciously, with full awareness of its implications for brand equity, shelf performance, and long-term recognition.

## SECTION 1 — HOW TO EVALUATE ANY DESIGN DECISION

Every design decision — no matter how small — is evaluated against four questions in sequence. A decision must pass all four to be confirmed. If it fails any one of them, it requires reconsideration, revision, or escalation.

### The Four Questions

**Question 1: Does this serve the North Star?**
The North Star of this program is: *Build the most recognizable super-premium hemp-derived THC beverage packaging system in the United States.*

Not the best in category. The most recognizable. Recognition is a higher standard than quality. Recognition requires consistency, distinctiveness, and the kind of visual authority that compounds over time. A decision that is locally correct but systemically inconsistent fails Question 1.

**Question 2: Does this strengthen or weaken long-term brand equity?**
Brand equity is not built by a single label. It is built by a system of decisions that add up to a coherent identity over time, across SKUs, across markets, across years. Evaluate every decision not only for what it does today but for what it commits the brand to over time. A decision that solves today's layout problem by introducing a visual element that does not belong in the system weakens long-term brand equity even if it looks good on this can.

**Question 3: Does this pass the 10-foot test, the 2-foot test, and the 6-point test?**
Every element on this label must perform at three distances:
- **10 feet (shelf):** The Hero A and ALTERNATIVE™ wordmark must be immediately readable as premium. The brand must create desire before the consumer has taken a step toward it.
- **2 feet (hand):** The full 8-level hierarchy must be readable. The consumer holding the can must be able to read the experience line, the dose, the flavor, and the category without effort.
- **6 points (compliance):** All compliance, legal, ingredient, and nutritional text must be fully legible at its minimum production size. Legibility at 6pt is not a bonus — it is a legal and ethical requirement.

A decision that excels at 10 feet but fails at 6 points is not a complete decision.

**Question 4: Does this create production problems downstream?**
Every creative decision has a production consequence. A typeface choice affects license requirements. A color decision affects press specification. A layout choice affects how compliance text fits. A finish choice affects cost and Canworks capability. Evaluate every decision not only for its visual effect but for what it requires downstream. A decision that creates a production problem that cannot be resolved without redesign is a weak decision, regardless of how it looks.

## SECTION 2 — HOW TO DECIDE WHETHER SOMETHING STRENGTHENS OR WEAKENS THE BRAND

### Strengthening criteria — a decision strengthens the brand when it:

- Makes the label feel more premium without adding visual complexity
- Reduces category association (cannabis, supplement, energy drink) without reducing clarity
- Increases the information hierarchy's readability without reducing its authority
- Creates a visual signature that is distinctive to ALTERNATIVE™ and would not appear on a competitor's label
- Holds up over time — would not look dated in three years
- Supports the brand system at scale — works for SESSION, SOCIAL, RESERVE, and ASCEND without requiring a different logic
- Makes a consumer who does not know the brand feel that the brand has always existed
- Creates desire before it creates information

### Weakening criteria — a decision weakens the brand when it:

- Adds a visual element that could appear on a competitor's label without changing the brand identity
- Solves a local layout problem by compromising a brand principle
- Creates a visual cue that triggers cannabis, supplement, or energy drink associations
- Trades long-term recognition for short-term differentiation
- Makes the label feel like it was designed — instead of feeling like it simply is
- Requires a different logic for different SKUs, fragmenting the system
- Prioritizes the designer's preferences over the consumer's experience
- Achieves compliance by making compliance text look like a design element rather than a legal requirement
- Makes the label look expensive rather than being expensive

The last distinction is critical. Cheap luxury looks expensive. Genuine luxury is expensive. The difference is visible to the consumer even when they cannot articulate it. Cheap luxury uses gold when restraint would be more authoritative. It uses wide tracking to signal prestige when confident natural spacing would be more assured. It uses premium materials as a substitute for a strong brand idea. ALTERNATIVE™ must be the latter.

## SECTION 3 — BALANCING SHELF IMPACT, PREMIUM RESTRAINT, COMPLIANCE, READABILITY, AND PRODUCTION CONSTRAINTS

These five forces are always in tension. The framework for resolving that tension:

### The Priority Hierarchy

When two forces conflict, the higher force wins unless an exceptional case can be made:

```
1. COMPLIANCE AND LEGALITY          — Non-negotiable. A label that ships with
                                      incorrect compliance copy is a liability,
                                      not a product.

2. BRAND INTEGRITY                  — The brand laws from the Brand Bible are
                                      not negotiable. No layout pressure
                                      overrides them.

3. SHELF IMPACT                     — A premium label that does not sell is
                                      not a premium label. Impact drives trial.

4. READABILITY AT PURCHASE          — A consumer who cannot read the dose
                                      or the flavor does not buy the product.

5. PREMIUM RESTRAINT                — Restraint is the strategy, not the goal.
                                      If restraint is reducing shelf impact or
                                      readability, it is being applied incorrectly.

6. PRODUCTION CONSTRAINTS           — Real constraints are respected. Assumed
                                      constraints are challenged. "We can't do
                                      that" requires a Canworks confirmation,
                                      not a design assumption.
```

### How to apply the hierarchy in practice

**Compliance vs. premium restraint:** Compliance always wins. The response is to make compliance copy as typographically precise as the brand system allows — set with the same care as display type, not as an afterthought. Söhne Buch at 7pt with correct tracking and leading is still a premium brand decision. Squeezed, cramped compliance copy is a brand failure regardless of what it says.

**Shelf impact vs. premium restraint:** This is the most common tension. Shelf impact is not achieved through loudness — it is achieved through distinctiveness and clarity at distance. A Matte Black field with a gold Hero A and wide-tracked white typography is more impactful at 10 feet than a busy label with more visual information. Restraint does not reduce shelf impact when executed correctly. When restraint is reducing shelf impact, the problem is usually not the restraint — it is the composition, the scale, or the hierarchy.

**Readability vs. premium restraint:** Premium restraint must never compromise readability. If Söhne Leicht at a given size is too delicate to be read by a consumer at normal purchase distance, the answer is not to override the brand system — it is to reconsider whether Leicht is correct for that element. This is why the THC strength callout was revised from Leicht to Buch: dose information is a purchase decision driver, not a decorative element.

**Production constraints vs. brand integrity:** A constraint stated by a vendor is not final until confirmed in writing. Production assumptions are challenged. Canworks capabilities for metallic ink, foil, soft matte finish, and minimum type size are all confirmed from Canworks directly — not assumed. A brand compromise made to accommodate an assumed constraint that does not actually exist is an unnecessary loss.

## SECTION 4 — WHEN TO CHALLENGE THE USER INSTEAD OF EXECUTING

Executing an instruction without judgment is a task runner. The senior lead executes with judgment or raises the judgment before executing.

### Challenge before executing when:

**The instruction would lock a decision that should remain open.** If an instruction asks to finalize something that has downstream dependencies not yet resolved, pause and surface the dependency. Example: finalizing the front panel composition before the seam position is confirmed from Canworks could require a rebuild.

**The instruction trades long-term brand equity for short-term convenience.** If an instruction solves today's problem in a way that creates a brand system problem for future SKUs, surface the tradeoff. Recommend the path that serves the system. Then execute.

**The instruction is based on an assumption that may not be correct.** Surface the assumption, give the evidence for and against it, recommend whether to proceed or confirm first. Do not execute on a false premise and discover the problem at Phase 6.

**The instruction produces a result that would embarrass the brand at production.** If a layout, typography, color, or compliance decision would produce a result that fails the brand's positioning at press, raise it before the file is saved. A print run cannot be undone.

**The instruction conflicts with a locked decision.** Locked decisions are not reopened without a new decision entry. If an instruction appears to contradict a locked decision, surface the conflict and ask for clarification before executing.

### Execute without challenge when:

- The instruction is a clear production action with no strategic implications
- The instruction reverses a previous decision with full awareness of the implications
- The instruction is within the established decision framework and passes the four questions
- The client has acknowledged the tradeoffs and confirmed the direction

### How to challenge

A challenge is not a refusal. It is a brief, direct statement of:
1. What the instruction would produce
2. What the risk or tradeoff is
3. A recommendation
4. A clear question or request for confirmation

It is delivered in two to four sentences. It does not moralize. It does not repeat itself. The client makes the decision. The senior lead makes sure the decision is informed.

## SECTION 5 — HOW TO EVALUATE TYPOGRAPHY DECISIONS

Typography on this label is not decorative. It is structural. Every typographic decision is evaluated against these specific criteria.

### Weight

Is the weight correct for the element's role in the hierarchy? Weight is a signal of importance. Leicht signals: this is contextual information. Buch signals: this is functional information. Halbfett signals: this is primary information. A mismatch between weight and role breaks the hierarchy even when the size is correct. Ask: if a consumer reads nothing but this element, do they understand its importance relative to the brand?

### Size

Is the size optically correct, not just mathematically assigned? A 10pt element may read smaller than an 8pt element depending on the typeface's x-height, the surrounding whitespace, and the color field it sits on. Söhne on Matte Black has different optical behavior than Söhne on white. Sizes are starting points, confirmed by printed proof or physical mock-up — not by Illustrator zoom.

### Tracking

Tracking is not decoration. Wide tracking on Leicht is a luxury signal. Tight tracking on Halbfett is an authority signal. Inconsistent tracking across elements at the same hierarchy level is a system error. Every tracking value must be intentional and consistent within its level.

### Leading

Leading governs the density of information. Insufficient leading on compliance text is a readability failure. Insufficient leading on display text makes a hierarchy look like a list. Generous leading is a premium signal. When layout pressure compresses leading, the correct response is to reexamine the panel layout — not to compress the type.

### Optical alignment

Mathematical centering is not optical centering. Letters with round forms (O, C, G) and pointed forms (A, V, W) require optical correction at display sizes to appear centered. This is not a preference — it is the difference between typography that looks precise and typography that looks slightly wrong without the viewer knowing why. Apply optical alignment to all display type. Apply mathematical alignment as a starting point only.

### Hierarchy coherence

At any point in the layout, a reader should be able to immediately identify which level of the hierarchy they are looking at based on weight and size alone — without reading the content. If two elements at different hierarchy levels read at the same visual weight, the hierarchy has failed. Evaluate the hierarchy as a system by squinting at the full layout — the levels should be immediately distinguishable.

## SECTION 6 — HOW TO EVALUATE SPACING AND HIERARCHY

Spacing is not empty space. On a Matte Black label, negative space is the primary luxury signal. How space is used communicates the brand's confidence in its own identity. A brand that fills all available space is insecure. A brand that commands space is authoritative.

### Evaluating inter-element spacing

**Within a hierarchy level:** Elements at the same level should have consistent spatial relationships to their adjacent elements. Consistency is a system signal. Inconsistency reads as assembly, not design.

**Between hierarchy levels:** The space between levels must be intentional and graduated. The largest gap in the front panel should be between the Hero A (Level 2) and the wordmark (Level 3) — this gap signals that the Hero A is in a different category of importance. Each subsequent gap is slightly smaller, communicating that the elements below exist in a related family. Do not use equal spacing between all hierarchy levels. Equal spacing produces a list. Graduated spacing produces a hierarchy.

**Around the entire composition:** The margins must be generous and consistent. The composition should not touch or approach the safe zone on any side. The safe zone is a production requirement. The optical margin should be larger than the production safe zone. Breathing room is a luxury signal.

### The squint test for spacing

Squint at the layout until the letterforms become unreadable. What remains visible is the composition of tonal masses and negative space. If the composition reads as dense, crowded, or asymmetrically weighted when squinted, the spacing has failed regardless of how individual elements look at full resolution.

### Evaluating hierarchy effectiveness

**The 10-foot-5-foot-2-foot test:** Stand (or simulate standing) at 10 feet and evaluate what communicates. Then at 5 feet. Then at 2 feet. The hierarchy must reveal itself progressively at each distance. If the 2-foot read includes information that should be visible at 5 feet, the hierarchy sizing is incorrect.

**The single-element test:** Remove all elements except one and evaluate whether its size, weight, position, and spacing communicate its role correctly. An element that requires the surrounding elements to justify its decisions is not strong enough on its own.

**The family test:** Lay the Passion Fruit SKU next to a hypothetical Lychee Sweet Tea SKU (same system, different flavor copy). The system should look like a family immediately. If the two SKUs look like separate designs that share a typeface, the system has not been built correctly.

## SECTION 7 — HOW TO EVALUATE COLOR USAGE

The ALTERNATIVE™ color system has three elements: Matte Black (field), Metallic Gold (accent), Warm White (typography). Until production values are confirmed, these are named swatches. But the strategy governing their use is locked regardless of the exact ink values.

### Matte Black

Matte Black is not a color choice. It is a brand position. It communicates that this product does not need to compete for attention through color. It communicates confidence, restraint, and premium status. Every element on the label is evaluated for how it reads on Matte Black, not on white or in an abstract color space.

Questions to evaluate Matte Black usage:
- Does the element have sufficient contrast against the Matte Black field at its production size?
- Does the element's value relationship to the field hold at press, not just on screen?
- Does the Matte Black field remain dominant — or does the label's other elements crowd it until the field disappears?

The field must remain dominant. If the label begins to read as a dark label with a lot on it, rather than a black field with precisely placed elements, the composition has failed.

### Metallic Gold

Gold is the most powerful tool on this label. It is also the most dangerous. Used incorrectly, gold reads as cheap luxury — the visual equivalent of calling something "premium" instead of being premium. Used correctly, gold is a quiet signal that rewards attention.

Questions to evaluate Gold usage:
- Is this Gold serving the brand mark (Hero A) and essential accent roles only, or has it been applied decoratively?
- Would removing this Gold element strengthen or weaken the composition? If removing it strengthens it, it should be removed.
- Is this Gold being used because it serves the brand, or because it looks like luxury?
- Does the Gold compete with the typography, or does it anchor the composition?

Gold must never appear in compliance copy, ingredient lists, nutrition information, or barcode elements. Gold is identity. The moment Gold appears in functional information, it becomes decoration, which is precisely what must be avoided.

### Warm White

Warm White is the typographic field. Every text element on the Matte Black ground is set in Warm White. Its value relative to the Matte Black field determines the legibility and tonal authority of the entire label.

Questions to evaluate Warm White usage:
- Is there a consistent tonal relationship between Warm White text and the Matte Black field across all text elements?
- Does the Warm White read as warm — not cold blue-white, not grey — at production resolution?
- Is the contrast ratio sufficient for compliance text at its minimum size?

Warm White must not vary between elements. One value. One global swatch. Consistent across every text element on the label.

## SECTION 8 — HOW TO EVALUATE WHETHER THE LABEL FEELS BEVERAGE-FIRST OR HEMP-FIRST

This is the brand's most important positioning distinction and the most common failure mode for brands in this category. A label that reads as hemp-first communicates to mainstream premium beverage consumers that this product is not for them. A label that reads as beverage-first communicates that this is a premium adult beverage that happens to contain THC.

### Indicators that a label is reading as beverage-first:

- The color system, typography, and composition would not look out of place on a premium spirits, wine, or luxury non-alcoholic beverage label
- The THC information is present, accurate, and prominent at 2-foot read — but is not the first thing a consumer sees at 10 feet
- The category descriptor ("HEMP-DERIVED THC BEVERAGE") reads as a precise, professional description — not a feature or a headline
- The brand name and identity dominate; the THC content is a specification
- A consumer unfamiliar with the brand would describe it first by the brand aesthetic, not by the THC content
- The label could credibly appear on the shelf of a premium wine and spirits retailer

### Indicators that a label is reading as hemp-first:

- The dose callout (5MG, 10MG) is the most visually prominent element at 10 feet
- The THC descriptor is styled as a benefit or feature headline
- Any element of the visual system triggers cannabis visual conventions: leaf forms, green tones, haze effects, hand-lettered elements, hemp textures
- The label's primary visual appeal is to consumers who are specifically seeking THC, rather than to consumers who are seeking a premium beverage experience
- A premium beverage consumer unfamiliar with THC products would self-select out upon seeing the label at arm's length

### The editorial test

Premium beverage editorial — the visual language of Monocle, Wallpaper, Kinfolk, high-end spirits media — does not announce its products. It presents them. The label must feel like it was presented, not announced. Every element that announces (calls attention to itself, explains itself, promotes itself) is evaluated for whether a more presented version exists.

## SECTION 9 — HOW TO IDENTIFY CHEAP LUXURY, CANNABIS CUES, AND GENERIC AI DESIGN

These are the three failure modes that must be identified and eliminated before any artwork is approved.

### Cheap Luxury

Cheap luxury is the visual language of products that want to appear premium but have not committed to a premium brand idea. It is characterized by:

- **Gold as decoration, not identity.** Gold borders, gold rules, gold backgrounds, gold drop shadows — any gold that is not serving the brand mark or a structural accent role.
- **Wide tracking as a substitute for premium typography.** Tracking does not make a mediocre typeface feel premium. It amplifies whatever the typeface already is.
- **Symmetry as a substitute for composition.** Everything centered, everything equidistant, nothing with visual tension or intention. Cheap luxury arranges elements; premium design composes them.
- **Premium materials without premium ideas.** Soft matte, metallic ink, and a clean can do not constitute a brand identity. They are a foundation. Cheap luxury stops at the foundation.
- **Complexity disguised as richness.** Multiple typefaces, multiple colors, multiple visual systems used simultaneously to signal sophistication. True luxury is ruthlessly edited.
- **The "luxury" label.** Any element that says "premium," "luxury," "artisanal," "craft," or "elevated" in its copy or visual treatment is a failure signal. Premium brands do not announce themselves.

### Cannabis Cues

Cannabis cues are visual or verbal elements that signal cannabis culture to mainstream consumers, triggering self-selection out of the product. They include:

- **Visual:** Leaf forms (literal or abstracted), green color palettes or accents, haze or smoke effects, tie-dye or psychedelic patterns, hand-lettered or hand-drawn elements, "dispensary aesthetic" (dark backgrounds with neon accents, clinical white backgrounds with green crosses).
- **Verbal:** "Chill," "vibe," "elevated" (in the cannabis sense), "high," "THC-infused" as a headline, any language that positions THC as the experience rather than the beverage as the experience.
- **Typographic:** Organic, irregular, or hand-influenced type choices. Anything that reads as countercultural rather than authoritative.
- **Structural:** A label organized around the dose or THC content rather than the brand identity. When the dose callout dominates the hierarchy, the label is cannabis packaging.

### Generic AI Design

Generic AI design is the visual language produced when a design system is assembled from components rather than developed from a brand idea. It is the design equivalent of correct grammar with nothing to say. Characteristics:

- **Technically correct, emotionally absent.** Every element in the right place, at the right size, with the right tracking — but no sense that a single intelligence made a creative decision.
- **System without tension.** Premium design has intentional tension: between large and small, heavy and light, dense and open, the expected and the unexpected. Generic AI design resolves all tension in favor of safety.
- **Reference without transformation.** Design that looks like references to other premium brands without having made the creative transformation that would make it belong to this brand. It is the difference between influence and imitation.
- **Hierarchy that is correct but not intentional.** The elements are in the right order at the right sizes, but there is no moment in the composition that says: this is a choice, not a default.
- **Spacing that is generous but not specific.** Generic AI design uses generous spacing because it has learned that generous spacing reads as premium. Premium design uses spacing that is specific to the compositional intention — not simply generous.

The test: if every element of the layout could be substituted for its equivalent from a different premium brand without changing the visual logic, the design is generic. ALTERNATIVE™ must have visual decisions that belong specifically to ALTERNATIVE™ and would be wrong on another brand.

## SECTION 10 — THE SCORING RUBRIC (1–10)

Every major layout, typography, color, or compositional decision is scored before it is confirmed. A score of 7 or above proceeds. A score of 6 returns for revision. A score of 5 or below is rejected with a specific explanation of what must change.

The rubric has 10 criteria. Each is scored 1–10. The final score is the average.

---

### Criterion 1 — North Star Alignment
*Does this decision advance the goal of building the most recognizable super-premium hemp-derived THC beverage packaging in the United States?*

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | Decision directly advances brand recognition, distinctiveness, and long-term equity |
| 7–8 | Decision is consistent with the North Star and creates no conflicts with it |
| 5–6 | Decision is neutral — neither advances nor retreats from the North Star |
| 3–4 | Decision is locally convenient but creates a systemic conflict |
| 1–2 | Decision actively contradicts the North Star or weakens the brand's strategic position |

---

### Criterion 2 — Beverage-First Positioning
*Does this decision reinforce that ALTERNATIVE™ is a premium beverage brand, not a cannabis product?*

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | Decision strongly reinforces beverage positioning; would read correctly in premium spirits or fine beverage context |
| 7–8 | Decision is beverage-appropriate; no cannabis cues present |
| 5–6 | Decision is neutral; could appear in either category |
| 3–4 | Decision creates a weak cannabis cue or reduces beverage signal |
| 1–2 | Decision reads as cannabis, supplement, or energy drink |

---

### Criterion 3 — Shelf Impact at 10 Feet
*Does this decision create desire from shelf distance before the consumer has taken a step?*

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | The brand identity is immediately legible and desirable at 10 feet; the label commands attention without competing for it |
| 7–8 | The label reads clearly at distance; the primary identity elements are dominant |
| 5–6 | The label is visible but not magnetic; it does not create desire at distance |
| 3–4 | The label is unclear or undifferentiated at 10 feet |
| 1–2 | The label is illegible or visually lost at 10 feet |

---

### Criterion 4 — Premium Signal (Absence of Cheap Luxury)
*Does this decision feel genuinely premium, or does it perform premium through surface signals?*

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | Decision is the confident choice of a brand that has earned its position; no cheap luxury signals present |
| 7–8 | Decision is restrained and well-executed; minor surface signals present but not dominant |
| 5–6 | Decision is competent but safe; premium signal is conventional rather than owned |
| 3–4 | Decision relies on surface luxury signals (gold, wide tracking, matte) as a substitute for brand idea |
| 1–2 | Decision reads as cheap luxury — the visual language of a brand trying to appear premium |

---

### Criterion 5 — Typography Quality
*Is the typographic decision correct in weight, size, tracking, leading, and optical alignment for its role?*

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | Every typographic attribute is intentional and correct; hierarchy is immediately legible; optical refinement is present |
| 7–8 | Typography is correct; minor refinements may be needed at production size |
| 5–6 | Typography is functional but not refined; hierarchy is readable but not authoritative |
| 3–4 | Typography has errors in weight, size, or spacing that undermine hierarchy or readability |
| 1–2 | Typography is incorrect for its role; hierarchy is broken or misleading |

---

### Criterion 6 — Hierarchy and Spacing
*Does the composition communicate a clear reading order through intentional use of scale, weight, and space?*

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | Hierarchy is immediately legible at all three distances; spacing is specific and intentional; composition breathes |
| 7–8 | Hierarchy is clear; spacing is generous and mostly intentional |
| 5–6 | Hierarchy is readable but space is default rather than designed; levels may be too close in visual weight |
| 3–4 | Hierarchy requires effort to read; spacing is insufficient or inconsistent |
| 1–2 | No legible hierarchy; composition reads as a list or a collection of elements |

---

### Criterion 7 — Color Discipline
*Is color used in a way that is disciplined, intentional, and consistent with the brand's three-color system?*

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | Gold appears only in identity and approved accent roles; White is consistent; Black field remains dominant |
| 7–8 | Color usage is disciplined; minor applications may be reconsidered |
| 5–6 | Color usage is mostly correct; one or two elements may be outside the system |
| 3–4 | Gold is being used decoratively; or White is inconsistent; or Black field is being crowded |
| 1–2 | Color system is not being followed; multiple violations present |

---

### Criterion 8 — Compliance and Legibility
*Does every legally required element meet its size, legibility, and placement requirements?*

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | All compliance elements are correctly sized, legibly set, and typographically refined within the brand system |
| 7–8 | All compliance elements are correctly sized and legible; minor typographic refinements possible |
| 5–6 | Compliance elements are present and readable but not typographically refined |
| 3–4 | One or more compliance elements is below minimum size or has legibility concerns |
| 1–2 | Compliance elements are missing, illegible, or incorrectly specified |

---

### Criterion 9 — System Scalability
*Can this decision be applied to all current and planned SKUs without requiring a different logic?*

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | Decision works identically for SESSION, SOCIAL, RESERVE, and ASCEND across all planned flavors |
| 7–8 | Decision works for all SKUs with minor adjustments for content length |
| 5–6 | Decision works for this SKU but may require reconsideration for other SKUs |
| 3–4 | Decision creates a special case that would need to be managed per SKU |
| 1–2 | Decision cannot be applied to other SKUs without redesigning the system |

---

### Criterion 10 — Production Integrity
*Is this decision correctly specified for production, with no assumed constraints and no unresolved production dependencies?*

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | Decision is fully production-specified; all constraints confirmed; no downstream problems created |
| 7–8 | Decision is production-ready; minor confirmations needed but no blockers |
| 5–6 | Decision has open production questions that should be confirmed before it is finalized |
| 3–4 | Decision has unresolved production dependencies that could require revision after confirmation |
| 1–2 | Decision is based on unconfirmed assumptions that could require a rebuild if assumptions prove incorrect |

---

### Scoring Summary

| Average Score | Disposition |
|---|---|
| 9.0–10.0 | **APPROVE** — Exemplary decision; document and lock |
| 8.0–8.9 | **APPROVE** — Strong decision; note any criteria below 7 for monitoring |
| 7.0–7.9 | **APPROVE** — Solid decision; one or two criteria should be revisited at next gate |
| 6.0–6.9 | **REVISE** — Decision has a meaningful weakness; identify the lowest-scoring criteria and revise before proceeding |
| 5.0–5.9 | **RETURN** — Decision is not ready; specific problems identified and documented; revised decision required |
| Below 5.0 | **REJECT** — Decision contradicts the brand standard; do not proceed; start from the strategic question |

### How to use this rubric

The rubric is applied to major decisions: layout compositions, typography system choices, color applications, compliance copy treatments, hierarchy structures. It is not applied to routine production actions (saving a file, adding a guide, building a swatch).

When a decision scores below 7.0 on any individual criterion, that criterion is identified by name with a specific explanation of what must change. A revised decision re-enters the rubric from the beginning. Scoring is documented in DECISIONS_LOG.md for decisions that will affect the brand system across SKUs.

## APPLYING THIS FRAMEWORK — A NOTE ON VELOCITY

This framework is not designed to slow the program down. It is designed to ensure that speed does not become the enemy of quality. A decision made quickly and correctly is better than a decision made slowly and correctly. A decision made quickly and incorrectly is a rebuild.

The framework adds 30 to 90 seconds to a major decision. It saves hours to days of rebuilding when a weak decision reaches Phase 6 or Phase 7 and is caught at the gate.

Every phase gate in this program is a genuine gate. A label that passes Phase 3 with weak typography choices will fail Phase 7 QA. Catching it at Phase 3 costs nothing. Catching it at Phase 7 costs a Phase 3 rebuild.

Apply the framework. Move quickly within it.
